ESL Teacher's Guide: The Dangers of Copying Successful People

Based on a BBC Reel short film (BBC Reel / YouTube)

Overview: This three-minute BBC Reel short explains survivorship bias: the mistake of judging a whole group by its most successful members only. It opens with the familiar figures of Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, and Mark Zuckerberg, then uses a famous World War II story about airplane armour, a gym, an old car, and scientific publishing to show why copying successful people is risky. Students learn ten key terms, answer timestamped comprehension questions, and discuss where survivorship bias shows up in their own decisions.

Target LevelB2 (Upper-Intermediate) Class TypeESL / EFL
Skill FocusListening, Speaking, Vocabulary, Critical Thinking Video Length3:18
Suggested Time60–75 minutes SourceBBC Reel / YouTube

Teacher Preparation Notice

  • Pre-teach the vocabulary in Section 2 before playing the video.
  • The video is short, so play it once straight through, then replay the sections at the timestamps in Section 3B for detailed questions.
  • Require students to support their discussion and comprehension answers using specific evidence from the video.
  • Note for accuracy: the transcript mishears two names. The statistician is Abraham Wald (not "Walt"), and the 2012 book is Michael Mauboussin's "The Success Equation" (not "Madison").

SECTION 1: Warm-Up Discussion Questions (10–12 minutes)

Discuss the following questions:

  1. Name a successful person you admire. Why do you admire them?
  2. Do you think successful people have special qualities that "ordinary" people do not have?
  3. Have you ever tried to copy a successful person's habits or advice? What happened?
  4. Do you think a university education is necessary for success in life?
  5. Is success mostly hard work, talent, or luck? Explain your view.

Teacher Guidelines: Warm-Up Procedure

Have students discuss questions 1–3 in pairs for about five minutes to activate their background knowledge and loosen them up. Then bring the class together for questions 4–5 as a whole group. Write any useful emergent vocabulary on the board; the term "survivorship bias" will likely come up naturally and can be left for Section 2.

SECTION 2: Vocabulary Training – Pre-Watching (15 minutes)

A. Key Terms Matching Exercise

Answer Key: Section 2A

1 - F, 2 - A, 3 - I, 4 - D, 5 - E, 6 - G, 7 - B, 8 - J, 9 - H, 10 - C.

B. Important Phrases/Expressions from the Video

Review these eight important expressions that appear in the film.

  1. "learn from and copy the successful."
    Prompt: Who is one person you would genuinely want to learn from and copy?
  2. "look at the whole picture."
    Prompt: Think of a situation where it is hard to look at the whole picture. What gets in the way?
  3. "made it back" (the planes that made it back).
    Prompt: In what kind of situation would you say someone "made it back"?
  4. "they don't make them like they used to."
    Prompt: What product do you hear people say this about, and are they right?
  5. "gave up along the way."
    Prompt: Why do many people give up along the way when they start a new habit or goal?
  6. "take big risks."
    Prompt: Would you take big risks to start your own business? Why or why not?
  7. "ignore conventional advice."
    Prompt: Have you ever ignored conventional advice? What happened?
  8. "just dumb luck."
    Prompt: Do you think some successful people owe their success to just dumb luck?

Answer Key & Support: Section 2B (Meanings)

  • "learn from and copy the successful": to study and imitate people who have already succeeded.
  • "look at the whole picture": to consider all the data, including the failures you cannot easily see.
  • "made it back": returned safely; here, the planes that survived and returned to base.
  • "they don't make them like they used to": said about old objects that seem better built than new ones.
  • "gave up along the way": quit before finishing; the people who did not reach their goal.
  • "take big risks": to do something with a real chance of failure or loss.
  • "ignore conventional advice": to deliberately not follow the standard guidance most people accept.
  • "just dumb luck": nothing more than chance, with no skill or effort involved.

Teacher Note: Pronunciation

Draw attention to how the narrator uses pausing (chunking) and stress when delivering these phrases. Model the chunking and stress for the class, especially the contrast in "the planes that made it back" versus the planes that did not.

SECTION 3: Video Comprehension Questions with Timestamps (25–30 minutes)

Teacher Note: Play the video once straight through, then replay the sections below. Pause at the end of each section to let students write their answers.

A. General Understanding

  1. What is the main idea of the video?
  2. Which successful people are mentioned at the start?
  3. Who was Abraham Wald, and what was he asked to study?
  4. What final advice does the video give about learning from successful people?

Answer Key: Section 3A

  1. Trying to learn from and copy successful people is dangerous because of survivorship bias; you must look at the whole picture, not just the survivors.
  2. Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, and Mark Zuckerberg.
  3. Abraham Wald was a statistician who was asked to work out the best way to protect airplanes from being shot down.
  4. Consider the whole picture before trying to learn from the successful.

B. Detailed Questions by Section

0:00–0:31 Copying the Successful

  1. What quality do Steve Jobs's biographers highlight as a factor in Apple's success?
  2. What do Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg have in common, and how has the media reacted?
  3. What does the narrator say trying to copy the successful "comes down to"?

Answer Key: 3B(i)

  1. His passionate temper.
  2. Neither of them finished university, and this fact has attracted a lot of admiration in the media.
  3. Survivorship bias.

0:32–1:12 The World War II Airplanes

  1. What was the original approach of Wald's research group?
  2. What mistake did Wald identify in this approach?
  3. What did the bullet holes on the returning planes actually show?

Answer Key: 3B(ii)

  1. They looked at the planes that came back, saw where they were hit worst, and reinforced those areas.
  2. The planes they were looking at were only the survivors; the planes that were hit and never returned were being ignored.
  3. The holes showed where a plane could be hit and still keep flying — exactly the areas that did not need reinforcing.

1:14–1:46 Bias in Everyday Life and Science

  1. What does a gym usually share, and what does it not discuss?
  2. What do people say about a 40-year-old car that still runs?
  3. What problem does the video describe in scientific journals?

Answer Key: 3B(iii)

  1. It shares examples of people who changed their body shape in a short time, but it does not discuss all the people who gave up along the way.
  2. They say "they don't make them like they used to," ignoring all the other cars from that period that broke down and had to be scrapped.
  3. Journals prefer to publish studies with significant results, while studies that fizzled out or showed insignificant results are left unpublished.

1:48–2:28 Education, Temper, and Luck

  1. If you include everyone who did not go to university, what does the data show?
  2. According to the video, does a university education help? Support your answer.
  3. What do we not know about entrepreneurs with a temper who did not succeed?

Answer Key: 3B(iv)

  1. The average earnings for this group are lower.
  2. Yes. You do not have to finish university to earn a lot, but if you look at the whole picture, it clearly does help.
  3. We do not know whether these qualities actually helped, or whether the success was the result of other factors or just dumb luck.

2:29–3:07 The Lottery Story and Conclusion

  1. Whose 2012 book describes the lottery story?
  2. Why did the man believe a ticket ending in 48 would win?
  3. What three "maybe" ideas does the narrator list before giving the final warning?

Answer Key: 3B(v)

  1. Michael Mauboussin's book "The Success Equation."
  2. He said he dreamed of the number 7 for seven straight nights, and 7 times 7 is 48.
  3. That a temper can help success, that a lack of higher education can give an advantage, and that the number 7 can make you rich.

C. Critical Thinking Questions

  1. Why is the airplane story considered such a clear example of survivorship bias?
  2. How does the lottery story connect to the video's main message?
  3. Name another area of life where survivorship bias might mislead people, and explain how.

Answer Key: Section 3C (Sample Responses & Evidence)

  • Q1: Wald's team could only examine the planes that returned. The data they could see was incomplete by definition: the planes that crashed were never available for study. The holes on the survivors pointed to harmless damage, so the obvious conclusion was the opposite of the correct one.
  • Q2: (Multiple responses acceptable.) The man attributed his win to the number 7 and a dream, but winning was almost certainly chance. The video uses the story to show how easily people invent a reason after the fact, ignoring everyone who followed a similar superstition and lost.
  • Q3: (Multiple responses acceptable.) Students might mention music streaming, where only famous artists are visible while millions of failed ones are ignored; or start-up culture, where the few billionaire founders are studied and the many failed founders are forgotten.

SECTION 4: Discussion Questions (15–20 minutes)

Teacher Discussion Guidelines

Divide students into small groups. Assign each group one or two subsections below. Emphasize respectful listening, practising turn-taking, and supporting opinions with personal examples or examples from the video.

A. Personal Reflection

  1. Think of a goal you set for yourself. Did you look at the whole picture, or only at people who succeeded?
  2. Have you ever been misled by a success story? What happened?
  3. How much of your own success or progress do you think came from hard work, and how much from luck?

Sample Directions (A)

Students might share stories of a fitness or study goal where they compared themselves only to successful peers. For Q3, they should contrast effort with circumstances they could not control.

B. Cultural Perspectives

  1. Does your culture celebrate university dropouts who became rich, or is education more strongly valued?
  2. Are success stories presented differently in your country's media than in this video?
  3. Is admitting that luck played a role in success acceptable in your culture, or is it seen as an excuse?

Sample Directions (B)

Students from countries where education is highly valued might contrast with the video's admiration for Gates and Zuckerberg. Views on luck versus effort vary widely and often reflect broader cultural attitudes toward individual responsibility.

C. Practical Application

  1. When you read about a successful person, what questions could you ask to protect yourself from survivorship bias?
  2. How could a company use the idea of "looking at the whole picture" to make better decisions?
  3. If a friend asked whether to copy a famous entrepreneur's habit, what would you advise?

Sample Directions (C)

Answers might include asking how many people tried the same thing and failed, what the person's starting circumstances were, and what role timing or luck played. A company could study failed projects, not just successful ones, before copying a competitor's strategy.

D. Future Planning

  1. How will you apply this idea the next time you read an inspiring success story?
  2. What is one decision you are facing now where survivorship bias might be affecting you?
  3. How can you decide when a risk is worth taking, rather than following a famous example?

Sample Directions (D)

Students should describe a concrete step, such as searching for failure data before making a choice. For Q3, encourage them to weigh their own resources and circumstances against the full range of outcomes, not just the highlighted success.

ADDITIONAL TEACHER RESOURCES

Teacher Notes: Differentiation

  • For weaker B2 classes: Provide the transcript for Section 3B and treat it as a reading-while-listening task.
  • For stronger B2/C1 classes: Have students summarize the video in their own words before looking at the comprehension questions. Challenge them to argue the opposing side: that copying successful people is actually useful.

Homework / Extension Activities

  • Reflection Paragraph: Write a 150-word paragraph answering: "Describe a time you were influenced by survivorship bias and what you would do differently now."
  • Quote Response: Choose one line from the video and explain in a short paragraph whether you agree or disagree.
  • Mini-Presentation: Prepare a two-minute speech on another common cognitive bias (for example, confirmation bias or the availability heuristic).
  • Observation Task: Find one news article or advertisement this week that shows survivorship bias and be ready to explain it.
  • Action Plan (Optional Writing): Write a five-point plan for how you will make a more informed decision about a goal you are currently pursuing.
